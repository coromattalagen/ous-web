Common Mistakes Even Advanced Learners Still Make

OUS Academy Editorial · 8 min read · Updated September 2026

You can hold a fluent conversation, write clear emails, and score well on exams — yet a handful of small grammar slips keep appearing. These are not beginner errors. They are fossilized habits that survive long after the rules have been learned.

This article looks at the most persistent ones, explains why they stick, and offers practical ways to notice and correct them without turning every sentence into a grammar exercise.

1. Subject-verb agreement with complex subjects

When the subject is simple, agreement is automatic. When the subject is long or interrupted by phrases, the verb often matches the nearest noun instead of the real head of the phrase.

Incorrect: The list of required documents are on the website.
Correct: The list of required documents is on the website.

The head of the subject is “list”, not “documents”. The same pattern appears with “a number of”, “the majority of”, and “one of the students who…”.

Practical fix: Mentally strip the sentence down to the core subject + verb before you speak or write. Ask: “What is doing the action?”

2. Article use with abstract and countable nouns

Advanced learners usually handle “a/an” and “the” with concrete objects. Problems appear with abstract nouns and with countable nouns used in a general sense.

Incorrect: She has good knowledge of marketing.
Correct: She has a good knowledge of marketing. or She has good knowledge of marketing. (both possible, but the first is more natural when “good” is present)

Another frequent case:

Incorrect: Technology has changed the way we communicate. (often fine)
But: Incorrect: I need advice about the career. → Correct: I need advice about a career / about my career.

There is no single rule that covers every case. Exposure to well-edited writing remains the most reliable teacher.

3. Preposition choice after verbs and adjectives

Prepositions are largely arbitrary. Advanced learners often transfer patterns from their first language or over-generalize from similar English verbs.

  • depend on (not “of” or “from”)
  • interested in (not “for”)
  • responsible for (not “of”)
  • good at (not “in” for skills)
  • discuss something (no preposition) vs talk about something
Practical fix: Keep a short personal list of the 15–20 combinations you most often get wrong. Review it once a week. Spaced retrieval works better than re-reading rules.

4. Tense consistency in longer stretches

In short sentences the tense is usually correct. In longer narratives or explanations, speakers and writers sometimes shift tense without a clear reason.

Inconsistent: When I started the course, I was nervous. Then I realize that the teacher is very supportive…
Consistent: When I started the course, I was nervous. Then I realized that the teacher was very supportive…

The shift is especially common when the speaker is reporting past experience and then commenting on it in the present.

5. Countable vs uncountable nouns that look similar

Words such as “information”, “advice”, “furniture”, “equipment”, “research”, and “evidence” remain uncountable in standard English even when the idea feels countable.

Incorrect: She gave me many advices. / an information / two researches
Correct: She gave me a lot of advice. / some information / two research papers / two studies

Why these mistakes persist

Three reasons stand out from language-learning research:

  1. Lack of noticing. The errors do not block communication, so they rarely receive corrective feedback.
  2. Transfer from the first language. Patterns that feel natural in L1 keep reappearing under time pressure.
  3. Insufficient deliberate practice. Advanced learners often focus on new vocabulary or fluency rather than polishing high-frequency structures.

A simple improvement routine

You do not need a full grammar course. A short, focused routine is more effective:

  • Record yourself speaking for two minutes on a familiar topic once a week. Listen only for one error type.
  • When you write an email or message, spend 30 seconds scanning for the five patterns above.
  • Keep a personal “error log” of no more than ten items. Review it with spaced repetition.

Progress at advanced levels is less about learning new rules and more about making the correct forms automatic under real conditions.
