IELTS vs. TOEFL: Which Test Actually Fits You?

OUS Academy Team Exam Prep 7 min read

Home / Articles / IELTS vs. TOEFL

"Which test should I take?" is usually the wrong first question. The right first question is: who is actually going to read this score? IELTS and TOEFL measure similar things in very different formats, and for most learners, the decision comes down less to which test is "easier" and more to which format matches how you naturally work under pressure — plus what your target institution actually accepts.

The core format difference

IELTS was built around a more traditional, human-scored format. TOEFL was built from the start as a computer-based test, and it shows in how each one is structured.

  • IELTS offers a paper-based or computer-based option in most locations, and the Speaking section is a face-to-face conversation with a real examiner (or, in some computer-delivered formats, recorded but examiner-assessed).
  • TOEFL iBT is entirely computer-based. You speak into a microphone and your response is recorded for scoring, rather than speaking with a live person in the room.

This single difference changes the entire feel of the test. If speaking into a screen instead of a person makes you tense up, that's worth weighing seriously — it's not a minor detail.

Scoring systems: bands vs. a numeric scale

IELTS scores on a band scale from 0 to 9, in increments of 0.5, with an overall band that averages the four skills. TOEFL iBT scores on a points scale from 0 to 120, adding up points across four sections (roughly 0–30 each).

Neither scale is inherently harder to score well on — they're just different systems, and most institutions publish their required score in whichever system that particular test uses. The practical question isn't "which scale is more forgiving," it's "what score does the place I'm applying to actually require, on the test they accept."

Speaking and Writing: where the real difference lives

Reading and Listening sections in both tests are broadly comparable in difficulty and skill demands. The meaningful differences show up in Speaking and Writing.

  • IELTS Speaking is a conversation — the examiner can adjust tone, ask natural follow-up questions, and the interaction feels closer to a real discussion. Some learners find this less intimidating because there's a human responding to them; others find live conversation more stressful than speaking to a screen.
  • TOEFL Speaking gives you a prompt, a short prep time, and a strict response window, recorded with no live interaction. It rewards learners who are comfortable organizing thoughts quickly and speaking fluently without any conversational back-and-forth.
  • IELTS Writing includes a distinctive Task 1 where you describe a chart, graph, map, or process — a specific skill worth practicing on its own, since it's unlike general essay writing.
  • TOEFL Writing includes an "integrated" task where you read a passage, listen to a related lecture, and then write about how they connect — testing your ability to synthesize information from multiple sources under time pressure.

Which institutions tend to prefer which test

This has shifted significantly over the years, and today most major universities in the US, UK, Canada, and Australia accept both tests. That said, some general historical patterns still influence local habits and familiarity:

  • IELTS has traditionally been more dominant in the UK, Australia, Canada, and much of Europe, and for immigration purposes in several countries.
  • TOEFL has traditionally been more dominant for university admissions in the United States.

These patterns are guidelines, not rules — the only reliable source is the specific requirement page of the institution or visa authority you're applying to. Always check the current accepted-test list directly rather than assuming based on general reputation.

The best test for you isn't the one with the better reputation. It's the one whose format plays to how you actually think and perform under time pressure.

A practical way to decide

  1. Check acceptance first. Look up the exact test (and minimum score) your target university, employer, or visa program requires. If only one test is accepted, the decision is already made.
  2. If both are accepted, try a short sample of each. Most official test providers offer free sample questions. Do one IELTS Speaking prompt and one TOEFL Speaking prompt back to back, and notice which one felt less paralyzing.
  3. Consider your writing style. If you're comfortable describing visual data (charts, graphs), IELTS Task 1 won't be a hurdle. If you're stronger at synthesizing spoken and written information together, TOEFL's integrated writing task may suit you better.
  4. Think about test-day format. If speaking to a real person calms your nerves, lean IELTS. If speaking to a screen without live interaction feels less pressured, lean TOEFL.

Quick self-check

  • Have you confirmed which test your specific target institution actually requires?
  • Have you tried one free sample Speaking task from each test to compare how each format feels?
  • Do you know the minimum score you need — in the correct scale for the test you choose?
