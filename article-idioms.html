Idioms That Don't Translate — And What They Really Mean

OUS Academy Team Culture & Language 6 min read

Home / Articles / Idioms

The first time you hear "it's raining cats and dogs," the mental image is genuinely strange — animals falling from the sky. Idioms are one of the clearest signs of a learner translating word by word instead of thinking in English directly, because idioms were never meant to be decoded literally. Their meaning lives in the phrase as a whole, not in the individual words.

Why idioms resist literal translation

An idiom is a fixed expression whose overall meaning can't be predicted from its individual parts. "Kick the bucket" has nothing to do with buckets or kicking — it means to die. That disconnect is exactly what makes idioms hard: you can know every single word in the sentence and still have no idea what it means.

This happens because idioms usually originate from a specific historical image, trade, sport, or custom — and over time, the phrase survives long after most speakers have forgotten (or never knew) where it came from. Native speakers use these phrases automatically, without thinking about the literal image at all, which is exactly why they sound so puzzling to a learner hearing them for the first time.

Six common idioms, actually explained

"It's raining cats and dogs"

Meaning: it's raining very heavily.
There's no confirmed origin, though one popular theory points to old drainage systems where heavy storms would sometimes wash dead animals through the streets — a grim but memorable image that may have inspired the phrase. Whatever its true origin, today it's simply a colorful way to say "heavy rain," nothing more.

"Break the ice"

Meaning: to do or say something that eases tension or awkwardness, usually at the start of a social situation.
This one is closer to literal than most — it comes from ships literally breaking ice to clear a path through frozen water, opening the way for others to follow. "Breaking the ice" at a party does the same social job: clearing the way for easier conversation.

"Hit the books"

Meaning: to start studying, usually seriously.
Nobody is physically hitting anything. "Hit" here works the way it does in "hit the road" (start traveling) or "hit the gym" (start exercising) — an informal way of saying "begin doing something," attached to a specific activity.

"Under the weather"

Meaning: feeling slightly ill.
This phrase likely comes from sailing — sailors who felt sick would go below deck, literally under the ship's weather deck, to recover away from rough conditions. Today it simply means "not feeling well," with no ship required.

"Cost an arm and a leg"

Meaning: to be very expensive.
A vivid exaggeration — nobody is actually trading limbs — used to emphasize that something's price feels painfully high, the way losing a limb would be a painfully high cost in a very different sense.

"On the same page"

Meaning: to be in agreement, or to understand something the same way as someone else.
The image is straightforward once you see it: if two people are reading from the same page of a document, they're seeing exactly the same information — a clean metaphor for shared understanding.

You don't learn idioms by translating them. You learn them by meeting the same phrase enough times, in enough real situations, that the meaning arrives before the individual words even register.

Why memorizing idiom lists rarely works

A list of a hundred idioms with their definitions looks efficient, but it usually fails for the same reason vocabulary lists fail: it builds recognition, not natural recall. You might remember "break the ice" means to ease tension when you see it on a quiz — and still completely miss it, or misuse it, in a real conversation three weeks later.

Idioms are learned far more durably through repeated, real exposure — hearing them used naturally in shows, conversations, and books, in situations where the context makes the meaning obvious even before you know the phrase. That contextual guessing is what makes the phrase stick.

A better way to learn idioms

  • Don't hunt for idioms — notice them. When you're watching or reading something in English and a phrase doesn't make literal sense, pause and look it up right there, in that context, rather than studying idioms as a separate, disconnected topic.
  • Learn the situation, not just the definition. "Break the ice" isn't just "ease tension" — notice that it's specifically used for the start of an interaction, often with strangers. That situational detail is often more useful than the dictionary definition alone.
  • Use new idioms deliberately, soon after learning them. Even in a slightly forced or unnatural sentence, using a new idiom yourself is what turns passive recognition into active vocabulary.
  • Don't translate idioms from your first language into English. Nearly every idiom has to be learned as its own fixed phrase — trying to build an English idiom out of a direct translation from another language almost always produces something a native speaker won't recognize.

Quick self-check

  • Next time you hear a phrase that doesn't make literal sense, write it down along with the situation it was used in — not just the definition.
  • Pick one idiom from this article and use it in a real sentence about your own life today.
  • Notice: do you already have an idiom in your first language for the same idea? How different is the image?
