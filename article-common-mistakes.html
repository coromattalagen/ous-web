Common Mistakes Even Advanced English Learners Still Make

OUS Academy Team Grammar & Usage 8 min read

Home / Articles / Common Mistakes

There's a strange stretch in learning English where the mistakes get quieter but don't disappear. You've moved past beginner errors — you're not mixing up "he" and "she," you're not saying "I am agree." But a smaller, stubborn set of mistakes survives. They survive because they don't sound obviously wrong. They survive because native speakers rarely correct them in conversation. And they survive because most textbooks treat them as a single lesson early on, then never return to them.

This is a walk through the mistakes that tend to outlast everything else — the ones showing up in otherwise fluent writing and speech from learners who have clearly put in the work. None of these are about talent or "natural ability." They're about specific patterns in English that don't map cleanly onto how most other languages handle the same idea.

1. Subject-verb agreement with collective and quantity nouns

Most learners master basic subject-verb agreement early: "she runs," not "she run." The trouble starts with nouns that describe a group or a quantity, because English is inconsistent about whether they take a singular or plural verb.

  • Collective nouns like team, family, and government usually take a singular verb in American English ("The team is winning") but can take a plural verb in British English when the group is acting as individuals ("The team are arguing among themselves"). Neither is universally "correct" — but mixing the two within the same piece of writing reads as inconsistent.
  • "Each," "every," "none," and "neither" pull the verb toward singular even when the sentence feels plural: "Each of the students has a copy," not "have." "None of them is ready" is the traditionally correct form, though "none of them are ready" is now so common it's widely accepted — but only in speech and casual writing.
  • "A number of" vs. "the number of" is a genuinely tricky pair. "A number of students are absent" (plural — you're talking about the students). "The number of absences has increased" (singular — you're talking about the number itself).

2. Prepositions that don't translate

This is probably the single largest source of small, persistent errors, because prepositions in English attach to specific words by convention, not logic. There's no rule that predicts them — you simply have to know that it's:

  • depend on, not "depend of" or "depend from"
  • married to someone, not "married with"
  • interested in, not "interested for" or "interested to"
  • good at something, not "good in"
  • arrive at a building or event, but arrive in a city or country

These pairings often get borrowed directly from a learner's first language, which is why the same preposition mistake tends to appear consistently for speakers of a particular language background. There's no shortcut here except exposure — reading and listening enough that the correct pairing starts to sound right on its own, before you can explain why.

3. Articles before abstract and uncountable nouns

Learners from languages without articles (like Russian, Japanese, or Korean) or with different article systems often over- or under-use "a," "an," and "the" — especially with abstract or uncountable nouns.

  • "I love the music" (wrong, if you mean music in general) vs. "I love music" (correct — no article for a general, uncountable concept)
  • "She gave me an advice" (wrong — "advice" is uncountable) vs. "She gave me a piece of advice" or simply "She gave me advice"
  • "I want to become the doctor" (wrong, unless one specific doctor is meant) vs. "I want to become a doctor"

The general pattern: use "the" when you and your listener both already know exactly which one you mean. Use "a/an" when you're introducing something new or general. Use no article at all with plural or uncountable nouns when speaking generally.

4. The confusable pairs that spellcheck won't catch

These aren't grammar mistakes exactly — they're mistakes that survive because the words sound alike or look alike, and because a spellchecker sees a correctly spelled word either way.

  • affect / effect — "affect" is almost always a verb ("this will affect the results"), "effect" is almost always a noun ("this had a big effect").
  • its / it's — "its" shows possession ("the company changed its policy"), "it's" is short for "it is" or "it has."
  • than / then — "than" compares ("faster than me"), "then" relates to time or sequence ("first this, then that").
  • lose / loose — "lose" is what happens to a game or your keys; "loose" describes something not tight.
  • whose / who's — "whose" shows possession, "who's" is short for "who is."

Because these pairs are usually pronounced the same or nearly the same, they rarely get corrected in spoken conversation — which means they can persist for years in writing, unnoticed, even at a genuinely advanced level.

5. Word order in indirect (embedded) questions

Direct questions in English invert the subject and verb: "Where is she?" But when that same question is embedded inside a larger sentence, the inversion disappears — and this is one of the most common errors even in advanced speech.

  • Wrong: "I don't know where is she."
  • Right: "I don't know where she is."
  • Wrong: "Can you tell me what does it mean?"
  • Right: "Can you tell me what it means?"

The rule: once a question is embedded inside another sentence ("I don't know...", "Can you tell me...", "I wonder..."), the word order goes back to normal statement order — subject before verb.

The mistakes that survive the longest aren't the ones you don't understand — they're the ones you understand perfectly and still produce automatically, because the wrong version has become a habit.

Why these particular mistakes are so persistent

There's a simple reason all five categories above are unusually stubborn: they rarely break communication. A listener understands "I don't know where is she" perfectly well, so there's no natural correction loop the way there is with a genuinely confusing sentence. The error gets rehearsed instead of fixed, over and over, in real conversations where nobody has the time or social permission to interrupt and correct it.

That's also exactly why they're worth deliberately hunting down. Fluency isn't just about being understood — it's about the gap between what you're understood to mean and how naturally that meaning arrives. Closing that gap usually isn't about learning something new. It's about noticing what's already slightly off and deciding to actually fix it, one pattern at a time.

Quick self-check

  • Read a paragraph you wrote recently. Circle every "its/it's," "than/then," and "affect/effect" — check each one deliberately.
  • Pick one preposition pair you're unsure about (depend on? interested in?) and look up ten real example sentences using it.
  • Next time you ask an embedded question out loud ("I wonder where..."), pause and check the word order before finishing the sentence.
